The title is a bit vague because it's hard to think of the write phrasing to express this idea. In my life I have many things which I like to read for their own sake. This post is regarding all the other reading that happens in my life (aside from incidental reading such as reading food labels or other day to day things). Imagine reading a cookbook or someone reading self-help or so on. When this sort of work is written, it's usually written in a fairly general tone. For example &ldquo;most beginner chefs hold their knives wrong, leading to less control&rdquo; or &ldquo;many people are unable to live up to goals they set due to them being unrealistic from the get-go&rdquo;. Some books will address you directly, but even then it is moreso a register then a direct address to you; most of these books are happening in front of you rather than speaking to you. The problem is that for reading that is not intrinsically motivated, you are not there to learn about the subject. You are there to learn the skills the book contains. If there seems to be no distinction between these notions, recall the thought experiment of Mary's room (see Wikipedia if you don't know it). For subjects like self-help it's cliche at this point for someone to read all about them and then never actually practice what they read, but this phenomenon can extend much further than this.

Let me give an example in a completely unrelated subject. If you are reading on proper technique for the application of makeup, you can read all of the guides on the internet you want, but you would probably be aided better by picking the first guide you see, reading it somewhat closely, then just trying it out, and coming back for different guides if this one fails. To actually learn a skill you need this trial and error. It won't lead to great results at first, but so long as you do it in the evening or wash it off before attending whatever serious engagements you might have that day, nobody will be the wiser if you aren't good at it. Indeed, the greatest progress can be made when *you* aren't the wiser that you are bad at something. Obviously correction requires honing your eye and seeing ways in which you can improve&mdash;this I do not dispute&mdash;but on the whole, knowing exactly how bad you are at something will likely be demotivating.

But if this thought ended here, I would just be recounting thoughts others have expressed in greater fidelity in the past, and once again it is slightly cliche in moral. Instead, my main interest in writing this article was a slightly different phenomenon from the above. The internet is full of&mdash;in the Stirnerian terminology&mdash;spooks. The netizens stress that you ought to care about this, that you need to believe in that, so on and so on. I don't just mean in the explicitly political arenas (this is where it is most obvious) but everywhere else too. Someone on the internet will discuss ultra-processed foods, and now the concept of &ldquo;ultra-processed foods&rdquo; has entered your life. The goal of all of these things is to attach their ideas into your brain. They hope for their favoured concept to ossify and become commonplace (well I shouldn't ascribe intentions to others, but certainly you get this sense while reading many works). In this sense, it is the ideological counterpart to the philosophy of &ldquo;fixing a problem you didn't know you had&rdquo;. It is the other extreme to hermeneutical injustice; the assault of one's life by innumerable meaningless concepts.

And these ideas do not even help you in your life. If there were an actionable next step, maybe it wouldn't be so bad, but for many concepts (think urbanism, where learning all about urbanism online typically does not actually prepare you to aide urbanist causes in real life, because actually effecting political change is much harder than just believing the ideology conveyed). I'll use an analogy of internet friendships. While people say time and time again that internet friendships are real, I think they are half friendships. The connection you may feel to another might be real and no different from someone in person, but there is a limit to how close you can get to them. You may like this online friend a lot, but they will never bring you soup when you're sick. They may provide conversation when you're lonely, but they'll never be there to quell it by just existing nearby while you two do different things. Maybe there are ways to bridge these gaps and approach a true friendship, but there's always necessarily a limit.

The only way to reclaim your life is to avoid these sorts of ideas. You must reject Russell's notion that we should expand the world of our thought, and live a small life for yourself. Because *every* life is small. Russell too, while speaking about the universe and existence and the limits of logic was just a man. You gain nothing from living too big of a life aside from a jaded outlook once you start to notice your inability to actually affect the subjects of your thought. Unless you truly enjoy reading on a subject, don't read on it unless it is of direct importance to your life. Think small.
