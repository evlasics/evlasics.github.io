I wanted to write a blog post, but I had nothing to say. I was in the mood for writing, so I devised a kind of pseudo-intellectual topic (as usual) but I realized that not everything has to be so serious. It's *my* blog, I can just fill it with pages upon pages of fluff, and nobody can stop me (I say this as though this is not what I have already been doing). On this note I think there is liberty associated with not having a predetermined topic either. Most of my other posts I just felt like writing, and ended up going off the rails, then typing about something else, and had to delete that&mdash;I believe the lack of focus shines through regardless, but to a lesser extent.

On the topic of random topics (pay no mind to the contradiction), I was thinking that school&mdash;high school and middle school&mdash;really are very valuable. Don't get me wrong, what a slog, having to sit through that. This is why I decided to graduate after Grade 11, as I really just hated it there. But I think there was a lot of joy in being given random stuff to study that I didn't necessarily *like*. The beauty was that I had to read stuff that was different from what I would personally choose. I don't know if I would've read Hamlet by now if not for high school, but I loved it. I don't remember any of Romeo and Juliet or The Twelfth Night though, and I regret this. Though I'm not going to scorn my past self&mdash;if I really cared, I'd just pick up the books now, I suppose. Which I *have* been meaning to do, by the way. But it's really hard to get through this kind of literature in your own time with no deadline or such, in part because you need to exert effort to understand it. Something which is shallow and immediately comprehensible is easily read, and not very rewarding, but the process of exerting effort to read is rewarding and difficult. In high school you have to finish before some time frame or you're behind, but afterwards you have the liberty to just not continue. And if you just don't continue, it will never get done.

Over time I realize that I have done a very good job cultivating some sense of self-discipline. Upon writing this out I've noticed that others have done better than me, just in different areas of life, but I don't think it negates what I am about to say. As a kid I was first discovered the joys of tying oneself to the mast of your ship to avoid the siren calls of activities which you rationally judge to not be valuable. What the id craves is not the same as what the superego values, after all, and in the modern world where *everything is addictive* you will not have the willpower to resist everything. I don't mean to say that you should be a perfect ascetic (nobody is), but that there are activities which I think net you almost zero value, and it is your imperative to figure out ways to avoid them to the extent you can&mdash;even when your willpower is insufficient. The earliest example I can recall is back when I was in high school (Grade 10?), I would scroll youtube shorts. But I judged this to be the lowest value activity one can engage in, so I found an alt client to youtube which allowed you to block shorts, then I stopped watching them. My schemes grow ever more sophisticated over the years, where at this point I've accepted the dialectical struggle associated with inventing one's own restraints. This is the reason why I manage not to use any social media, not due to my incredible willpower (or lack of an account) but because I simply prevent myself from doing so. In an era where social media has ended up being a very negative force on society, the ascetic impulses of my teenage brain struggling to cope with (as of then) undiagnosed ADHD have really saved me.

As time passes further, yearn more and more for a world without digital technology. I mean naturally I reap the benefits, but I think reading was truly one of the greatest things to happen to mankind, and as technology has basically killed reading (most modern books are human-created slop), I hate it a lot. Of course I cope sometimes about why I cannot get rid of my own devices. Obviously I need a computer&mdash;without a computer you can't really be in math, as you need to, at some point, latex your work&mdash;but at the same time, I'm thinking about it, and if I didn't have a computer I would go to the library much more regularly. After all, where is one to learn about all the stuff I learn about if not at the library? I would need the math library for my math work, and the regular library for any other curiosities. It feels as though *because* everything can come to you instantly through the internet, you have no need to go outside other than when you truly want to. Covid only exasperated this, where everyone was forced to go online, so everything adapted to this more online format, making everything available online to an even greater extent. Not to mention the hit to social cohesion this brings. If everyone's doing stuff in person, then you have no choice but to meet up with others in real life. When it's all online, no longer.

I sometimes wish I could just get rid of my internet access. But I can never return to the world where everyone else has as well. Put differently, in a terminally online generation, being entirely offline is untenable. Maybe I should join a book club.
