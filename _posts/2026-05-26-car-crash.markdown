&ldquo;The smell was strange. Sharp. Chemical. Wrong. The concrete wall was too close. My glasses were gone. One of my kids was standing on the sidewalk next to our car—not crying, just confused. The seat belt had held. The crumple zone had crumpled. The airbag had fired. Everything designed to protect bodies had done its job. But the car, a Tesla Model X, was totaled.&rdquo;

This is a passage from the April 2026 edition of The Atlantic (*My Self Driving Car Crash* by Raffi Krikorian on page 14) and while it's about specifically self-driving car crashes, I found this opening to be extremely similar to my experience with a car crash. So let me write some prose to describe the event from my perspective, then I'll talk a little bit about what I took from the whole event.

Back in the '24 Fall semester, I was living near Jane station in my grandfather's basement. This was a difficult semester for me. After getting phenomenal grades in first year, and even a 100 in the '24 Summer semester, I was having a hard time staying motivated in second year. The work seemed inane and the courses seemed pointless. After a week of not-so-dedicated studying I would visit my Dad in Burlington&mdash;about 2 hours via transit, as I had to go from Jane to Royal York to Mimico to Burlington to his house, but only about an hour if he drove me from a halfway point that was easy for both of us to get to.

Due to my dwindling motivation to do schoolwork, I would let it accumulate until it took form into a sword of Damocles that would linger over my head. This meant I would often let my apartment get in a pretty messy state, as the dread of the schoolwork turned the air into a thick syrup, making it difficult for me to move at all. So when my Dad phoned asking me if I would like to come over, I told him I have to tidy up my place, then I'll leave right after. The lights were dim, and the sky was already darkening. I filled the dishwasher, put away my papers, placed clothes in the laundry, then emerged from my cave, and headed towards our meetup point. Coincidentally it took approximately the same amount of time for both of us to get there. Well that's the story I went with at least. In actuality it was a bit faster for me, but I would use this time for extra cleaning, and we would still arrive at the same time, so it didn't matter so much.

Entering his car would always leave the stress of the week behind me. He always had nice music playing&mdash;which I would rarely listen to on my own&mdash;and he would greet me warmly, then we'd head to his place to watch TV, eat snacks, drink tea, really everything you could want in life. I was talking with a friend online about something unimportant as we headed out from the station. I was used to our route home, and without having to look at the road I could tell roughly where we were just by using the corner of my eye, without taking my eyes off of whatever my friend was saying. From this window I tried to gauge where we were, and saw a car getting bigger, bigger, bigger, bang.

The airbag was soft. There was a smell of smoke&mdash;smoke I had only smelled while soldering before. Looking up, the cabin was filled with smoke. There was a sense of unreality, I couldn't feel anything definite. I knew in that moment I was supposed to feel worried for my father, but I can't say in earnest that I felt anything more than just confusion. I pieced together in my mind what had happened, which direction the car had hit us from, and with this delay, the worry set in. I started to panic. Is he alive? It hit his side of the car. It looks like the car was crumpled, are his legs okay? What is this smoke? Is the car about to explode? From this whirlwind of thoughts, only one question managed to escape my lips&mdash;one ever so inadequate of expressing my true intentions: &ldquo;Are you okay?&rdquo; His response took a while, but it felt immediate. &ldquo;Yeah I'm fine.&rdquo; &ldquo;You're not hurt?&rdquo; &ldquo;No, I'm perfectly fine.&rdquo; In retrospect, he must have asked me if I was okay, but I can't even remember this. We discussed what on earth was going on, then exited the car. His door wouldn't open, so he had to crawl out through my side, and there we were, in one of the busiest intersections in Toronto, in the freezing cold, standing up beside a totalled car.

A philosopher of our time; a true scholar, unparalleled in both his wit and his tact, rolled down his window and shouted &ldquo;Move your car!!&rdquo; as we both stood there stunned. Of course, trying to move our car would be a fool's errand, as with the smoke, it was time to move *away* from the car. But his input was duly noted by the both of us. We headed to the corner of the crosswalk where the other car had parked, and the driver apologised profusely and made sure nobody needed to go to the hospital. I felt a bit bad for her, because she was driving a BMW, so probably her insurance premiums were going to skyrocket. Until she explained what had happened, and then I felt a little bit less bad. She said she was in the left lane signalling left, but she saw us coming and figured we were going right and so she turned right. Of course, we were headed straight, so then she crashed into us. Why on god's green earth she decided to turn right after signalling left, I still cannot tell you to this day. There were tow-trucks at the intersection where we crashed. I don't mean they arrived, I mean they were already there. They moved the car, dealt with all of the stuff, and our job was only to enter their truck and head with them to the collision center. While they were attaching his car to the tow truck, we called my Grandmother and I led with &ldquo;Nobody is injured, but we got into a car crash.&rdquo; Her first question was &ldquo;Is anyone injured?&rdquo; which somewhat irritated me. My Dad started to panic about whether or not he would have to pay for the towing, and I reassured him that his insurance would cover all this. Right before we left, a very kind man stopped and gave us his dashcam footage of the incident. 

We headed to a crash center to report the incident, get a temporary car, and then finally headed home. He and I both agreed that it was sad to watch his car go, because we had liked that car. But it was time to say goodbye. Even though the damage was minimal, resetting the airbags was expensive enough that most insurance companies would rather you just buy a new car. At the crash center I felt a very minimal pain in my spine. I was on the phone with my sister then. She advised me to go to the hospital, but I said I would go tomorrow if it was more severe, or if it ended up being persistent. Neither occurred, and I was perfectly fine.

The crash ended up being 0% my Dad's fault according to the insurance. We were going through a green light going straight in the &ldquo;go straight or turn right, your choice&rdquo; lane slightly below the speed limit, after all. So he got more compensation than he even expected he could have sold the car for, and he got a wonderful new car which we both love (A Hyundai Elantra) which has served him well ever since.

But my reflection on the incident is just how incredible the safety features of the car were. Had we not had seatbelts, airbags, a crumple zone, all the safety features that have gone into cars, one of us might have been seriously injured or dead. But we were not. It makes me very appreciative of all the people who have dedicated their life to adding safety requirements to car design, both designing and lobbying for these. It's because of their hard work that so many people survive car crashes today. So many people nowadays have experiences like Raffi Krikorian's, where they can walk away and say &ldquo;too bad about the car.&rdquo; Naturally, I am an advocate for reducing our dependency on cars. Deaths due to public transit are significantly less common than deaths due to cars, and public transit has a whole host of other benefits for people, cities, and their riders, as well as car drivers. But I still cannot help but feel thankful, in this one instance, for all the work that has gone into cars.
